## What is a Volcanic Arc?

A volcanic arc is a belt of volcanoes formed above a subducting plate. They appear curved—or 'arcuate'—when viewed from space because Earth is a sphere, and intersecting a sphere with a planar dipping slab produces a curved surface trace.

### Types of Volcanic Arcs

1. **Island Arcs (Oceanic-Oceanic Convergence):** When one oceanic plate subducts beneath another oceanic plate, magma rises through oceanic crust to form chains of volcanic islands (e.g., the Aleutian Islands or the Mariana Islands). Lavas range from basaltic to andesitic.
2. **Continental Arcs (Oceanic-Continental Convergence):** When an oceanic plate subducts beneath a buoyant continent, ascending magma must traverse thick (30–70 km) continental crust. As magma pools in crustal chambers, it melts surrounding country rock and undergoes fractional crystallization. This enriches the magma in silica (SiO₂), producing viscous and volatile-rich **andesite, dacite, and rhyolite**. The **Sunda Arc of Indonesia** (Sumatra and Java) and the **Andes of South America** are premier global examples.

### Why Volcanic Arcs are Explosive

Unlike the fluid, effusive basaltic lava flows of Hawaii or Iceland, volcanic arc lavas are thick and sticky. Their high viscosity prevents gases like steam (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) from escaping smoothly. As magma nears the surface and confining pressure drops, gas bubbles expand violently, shattering the magma into volcanic ash, pumice, and catastrophic pyroclastic density currents.
