## What is a Subduction Zone?

A subduction zone marks a convergent plate boundary where one tectonic plate bends and slides beneath another, descending into Earth's mantle. Because oceanic lithosphere is cold and composed of dense mafic rocks (basalt and gabbro) capped by sediment, it becomes denser than the warm, buoyant asthenosphere beneath it as it ages.

Once an oceanic plate cools for more than ~20 to 30 million years, it becomes gravitationally unstable, eventually plunging into the mantle along deep oceanic trenches such as the **Java Trench** (reaching depths over 7,400 meters south of Indonesia) or the Mariana Trench.

### The Wadati-Benioff Zone

Subduction is unique in producing Earth's deepest earthquakes. While most intra-crustal earthquakes occur in the brittle upper 20 to 30 km of Earth, the cold interior of a subducting slab remains brittle and capable of seismic fault rupture down to depths of **600 to 700 kilometers**. This dipping planar zone of deep seismicity is known as the **Wadati-Benioff Zone**.

### Role in the Global Volcanic Arc System

As the descending slab sinks, pressure and temperature trigger metamorphic reactions that release water into the overlying mantle wedge. This volatile release triggers **flux melting**, fueling the curvilinear chains of stratovolcanoes known as volcanic arcs.
