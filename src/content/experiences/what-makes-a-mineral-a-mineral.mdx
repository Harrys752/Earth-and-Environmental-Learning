## Beat 1: The Global Nickel Giant

Indonesia produces more nickel than any other nation on Earth—accounting for **over 60% of the entire global supply** in 2024. This raw material is critical for modern stainless steel and the cathodes of electric vehicle batteries.

Yet, when geologists examine nickel ore from the hills of Sulawesi or Halmahera, they are not just looking at valuable economic commodities; they are studying **minerals**.

What actually turns a rock or chemical element into a "mineral" in scientific terms?

---

## Beat 2: The Five Strict Scientific Criteria of a Mineral

To a geologist, the word *mineral* has a very precise scientific definition. To be classified as a true mineral, a substance must satisfy **all five** of the following rules:

1. **Naturally Occurring:** Formed by natural geological processes without human intervention (synthetic laboratory crystals are not true minerals).
2. **Inorganic:** Not formed primarily from organic biological tissues (amber, coal, and wood are organic materials, not minerals).
3. **Solid at Standard Conditions:** Liquid mercury and water are not minerals, though ice is a mineral because it is solid water.
4. **Definite Chemical Composition:** Defined by an exact chemical formula or a bounded range of atomic substitution (e.g., Quartz is strictly SiO₂; Garnierite is (Ni,Mg)₃Si₂O₅(OH)₄).
5. **Ordered Internal Crystal Structure:** Atoms are arranged in a repeating, three-dimensional geometric lattice. Non-crystalline volcanic glass (obsidian) lacks this order and is called a *mineraloid*.

### Minerals vs. Rocks
The distinction is fundamental: **A mineral is a single pure crystalline compound, while a rock is an aggregate mixture of one or more minerals**. Granite, for example, is a rock composed of individual crystals of quartz, feldspar, and biotite mica.

## Beat 3: Physical Diagnostic Properties

Because many minerals look similar to the naked eye (color is often deceptive due to trace chemical impurities), geologists rely on systematic physical tests:

- **Mohs Hardness Scale (1–10):** Measures scratch resistance from Talc (1) to Diamond (10). Quartz (7) scratches glass; Calcite (3) is scratched by a copper coin.
- **Streak:** The color of the pulverized mineral powder when scraped across an unglazed porcelain plate. Pyrite looks like bright gold, but its streak is greenish-black.
- **Luster:** The way light reflects off the mineral surface—metallic (shiny like chrome), vitreous (glassy like quartz), or earthy (dull like clay).
- **Cleavage vs. Fracture:** How a crystal breaks along atomic planes of weakness. Calcite cleaves cleanly into rhombohedrons, while quartz fractures with smooth curved surfaces (conchoidal fracture).
