## Beat 1: The Sinking Megacity

Parts of **Jakarta**, home to over 10 million people, are sinking faster than almost any other coastal metropolis on the planet. Along the northern coast in districts like Muara Baru and Ancol, the ground has dropped by **up to 12 centimeters per year**.

Today, roughly **40% of Jakarta's land area sits below sea level**, held back from catastrophic daily flooding only by a concrete coastal sea wall that must continually be built higher.

Where is the ground beneath Jakarta actually going? Is the city simply too heavy, or is an unseen hydrological process pulling the foundation out from beneath the city?

---

## Beat 2: Aquifers and Pore-Water Pressure Mechanics

The soil beneath Jakarta is not solid rock; it is a stack of soft, unconsolidated sediment layers deposited over thousands of years by the Ciliwung and surrounding rivers:

1. **Unconfined Shallow Aquifers:** Near-surface sand and silt layers holding shallow water. In dense urban areas, this water is frequently polluted by surface runoff.
2. **Confined Deep Aquifers:** Deep sand and gravel layers (100–300 m underground) containing clean, pressurized freshwater.
3. **Compressible Marine Clay Aquitards:** Thick, dense clay layers sandwiched between the aquifers.

### The Physics of Pore Pressure
In an undisturbed aquifer, water fills the microscopic spaces (pores) between sediment grains. This trapped water exerts **hydrostatic pore pressure** that helps push upward against the immense weight of the overlying ground, buildings, and soil.

When millions of liters of deep groundwater are pumped out each day to supply industries, hotels, and households that lack piped municipal water, **pore pressure drops sharply**.

Without the upward hydraulic support of water, the full overburden weight of the city crushes the compressible clay layers. Microscopic clay flakes collapse and pack tightly together. The clay layer shrinks in thickness—and **the ground surface above sinks**.

## Beat 3: The Irreversible Trap of Clay Compaction

A critical geological reality of land subsidence is that **clay compaction is mechanically plastic and irreversible**.

Unlike a flexible elastic sponge that springs back when re-submerged in water, clay minerals realign into an interlocking, dense crystal structure once crushed under lithostatic pressure. Even if all groundwater pumping were to stop tomorrow, the compacted clay layers will **never expand back to their original thickness**.

The ground that has sunk into the sea is permanently lost to the ocean unless engineered barriers and artificial reclamation are maintained.
