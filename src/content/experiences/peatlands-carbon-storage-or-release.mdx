## Beat 1: The Fire Beneath the Earth

In the autumn of **2015**, satellite sensors tracking global atmosphere detected a shocking anomaly: over a period of just two months, fires burning across Indonesia released **more carbon dioxide into the atmosphere than the entire 28-nation European Union combined**.

Dense yellow-brown toxic haze blanketed Southeast Asia, grounding flights, closing schools across multiple countries, and triggering respiratory crises for hundreds of thousands of people.

These were not ordinary brush fires blazing through treetops. The flames were burning **underground**, consuming ancient organic soil that had been accumulating since the end of the last ice age.

How can wet swampy soil burn, and how do tropical peatlands transform from Earth's greatest carbon sinks into massive climate tipping points?

---

## Beat 2: The Anatomy of a Tropical Peatland

A **peatland** is a wetland ecosystem where dead plant material—leaves, branches, fallen tree trunks—does not fully decompose. Over thousands of years, waterlogged, low-oxygen (anoxic) conditions preserve this organic debris, building thick, sponge-like soil layers called **peat**.

- **Immense Thickness:** In parts of Kalimantan, Sumatra, and Papua, peat layers can reach depths of **up to 10 to 15 meters**.
- **Dense Carbon Storage:** While covering just 3% of the world's land area, global peatlands store **twice as much carbon as all the world's forests combined**.
- **Natural Hydrology:** A healthy, intact peatland is 90% water by weight. It acts like a giant, sodden sponge that absorbs torrential monsoon rains and slowly releases freshwater during dry spells.

## Beat 3: The Failure Chain — From Drainage to Subsurface Fire

When peatlands are targeted for agricultural expansion (such as oil palm or pulpwood plantations), developers dig extensive networks of **drainage canals** to lower the water table:

1. **Canal Drainage:** Canals carry away the trapped groundwater, lowering the water table by 1 to 2 meters.
2. **Aerobic Exposure:** As the water drains, atmospheric oxygen infiltrates deep into the spongy soil. The dead plant matter dries out into compressed, highly combustible fuel.
3. **Deliberate or Accidental Ignition:** Fires set cheaply on the surface to clear brush quickly penetrate downward into the dry peat.
4. **Subsurface Smoldering Combustion:** Unlike open flames that need strong wind, peat burns via low-temperature, oxygen-starved **smoldering combustion**. The fire creeps silently underground along dry root tunnels, burning for weeks or months beneath the surface and defying rain.
