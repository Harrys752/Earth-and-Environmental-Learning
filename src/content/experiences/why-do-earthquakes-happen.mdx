## Beat 1: The Opening Mystery of Palu 2018

> **Why did a single earthquake in Central Sulawesi in 2018 trigger a deadly tsunami and turn solid residential land into flowing mud—from a fault type that textbook geology says rarely causes tsunamis?**

On the evening of **28 September 2018**, a shallow **moment magnitude (Mw) 7.5** earthquake struck Central Sulawesi, Indonesia. Within minutes, over **4,300 lives were lost**, entire residential suburbs slid for hundreds of meters as liquefied mud, and 2-to-6-meter tsunami waves swept into the enclosed inlet of Palu Bay.

The epicenter lay along the **Palu-Koro Fault**, a major **strike-slip (transform) fault**. According to classical seismology, strike-slip faults slide horizontally past each other and do not displace huge vertical columns of ocean water to generate tsunamis. Why did Palu defy the simple textbook rules?

To answer this, we must understand the fundamental physical engine of earthquakes: **elastic rebound**.

---

## Beat 2: The Physical Engine — Elastic Rebound Theory

Earth's brittle crust is crisscrossed by fractures known as **faults**. Tectonic plate motions constantly push crustal blocks against each other at rates of several centimeters per year.

However, faults do not slide smoothly. Frictional resistance along rough contact surfaces (called **asperities**) keeps the fault locked tightly in place.

```
1. Continuous Tectonic Stress → Fault remains locked by friction.
                ↓
2. Elastic Strain Accumulation → Rock bodies bend, warp, and store elastic potential energy.
                ↓
3. Frictional Threshold Exceeded → Asperities fracture; locked fault suddenly slips.
                ↓
4. Seismic Energy Release → Elastic strain snaps back, releasing radiating seismic P and S waves.
```

When stored mechanical stress exceeds the shear strength of the locked fault plane, the rock fractures in milliseconds. The deformed rock snaps back toward its unstrained state—a phenomenon formulated by Harry Fielding Reid as **Elastic Rebound Theory**. The shock radiates through the crust as seismic waves that shake the ground surface.

## Beat 3: The Three Major Fault & Boundary Types

Different tectonic boundary configurations produce distinct fault kinematics, stress regimes, and earthquake depths:

1. **Transform / Strike-Slip Faults (Shear Stress):**
   - Blocks slide horizontally past one another (e.g., Palu-Koro Fault in Sulawesi, Great Sumatran Fault in Sumatra, San Andreas Fault in California).
   - Earthquakes are typically shallow (depth under 20 km), producing intense local surface shaking.
2. **Convergent / Reverse & Megathrust Faults (Compressional Stress):**
   - One crustal plate overrides another (e.g., Java Subduction Trench, Sunda Megathrust).
   - Capable of generating massive vertical seafloor displacements and open-ocean megatsunamis (e.g., 2004 Indian Ocean Mw 9.1–9.3).
3. **Divergent / Normal Faults (Tensional Stress):**
   - Crust pulls apart and drops down along extensional fault blocks (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift).

## Beat 5: Real-World Indonesian Anchor — The 2018 Palu Mw 7.5 Disaster

The Palu earthquake serves as one of the most thoroughly documented multi-hazard natural disaster sequences in modern earth science.

### Why Palu Liquefied and Flooded:
- **Massive Soil Liquefaction:** Saturated alluvial soils in the Palu valley lost all shear strength during prolonged shaking. Groundwater under pressure forced sand grains apart, converting solid ground into a viscous liquid slurry that flowed down gentle 1–2° slopes for over 1.5 kilometers. Recent peer-reviewed studies (NTU / Nature Geoscience, 2019) demonstrated that unlined agricultural irrigation aqueducts had elevated the local water table to near-surface levels, multiplying liquefaction severity.
- **The Secondary Tsunami Mechanism:** Scientists from Springer *Landslides* (2018) and JGEET verified that the tsunami was primarily generated by steep coastal and submarine sediment landslides collapsing into Palu Bay rather than vertical fault displacement alone.
